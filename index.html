02 · The market Start here · The arena, the prize, the gap

First, the market: $102.8bn is already being spent

Companies already pour serious money into training, coaching measurably moves quota, and sellers still get almost no live practice. That gap is the whole reason this coach exists. Every figure is dated and sourced, with the caveats the sources carry.

Round 2 · What coaching actually returns The scoreboard, with the famous fake number called out

+32% win rates

Consistent coaching pays

Organisations with consistent sales coaching and impact measurement report 32% higher win rates and 28% higher quota attainment than those without. A survey comparison, not a controlled trial.

Source: Korn Ferry, 5th Annual Sales Enablement Study (2023)
91.2% vs 84.7%

Formal coaching vs leaving it to managers

Overall quota attainment for companies with a formal coaching process versus an informal one, across 1,200 companies. Only 21% of companies had a formal process at all.

Source: CSO Insights, 2014 Sales Management Optimization Study
+43% → +67%

Training alone vs training plus coaching

Revenue performance lift from salesperson training alone, versus training combined with manager coaching, over 18 months. One large US travel-services company, 246 salespeople and managers, so an illustration, not a general rate.

Source: Wilson Learning, manager training study
>80%

Forgotten within 90 days

Participants in traditional curriculum-based training forget more than 80% of what they were taught within 90 days, per Harvard Business Review's summary of the field. The peer-reviewed root of this, Ebbinghaus (1885, replicated 2015), measured “savings” on relearning rather than recall: about 12.5% of the savings remained after 31 days.

Sources: HBR, “Your Sales Training Is Probably Lackluster” (Cespedes); Ebbinghaus via Solofire's source-checked statistics
About the famous “353% ROI” figure There is no verified study that gives a clean dollar return per dollar spent on sales training. The widely quoted 353% ROI ($4.53 back per $1) comes from Accenture's own company-wide learning programme, published in a 2006 book, and is routinely misattributed to sales training. It is not used on this page as a sales figure; the coaching and quota evidence above is what holds up.

Round 3 · What sellers had before this Five worthy opponents, one shared weakness: no live reps

MethodWhat it cost, and where it fell short
Classroom workshops & sales kickoffsKickoffs alone ran $1,000–$1,499 per salesperson, on top of the $1,000–$1,499 training spend (ATD, 2023). The format is the problem: intense, infrequent bursts with no practice in between, so the forgetting curve above takes most of it.
Manager ride-alongs & 1:1 coachingThe highest-leverage method in the research, and the scarcest. Managers spend on average 9% of their time developing direct reports (Gartner, 2019), 76% of firms say their managers provide too little coaching, and 70.8% of sales leaders coach each rep 60 minutes a week or less (Sales Management Association, 2018; CSO Insights, 2014).
Peer roleplayThe closest pre-AI analogue, limited by scheduling and who is free to play the buyer. 47% of Gen Z reps say they do not get enough role-play opportunities before customer calls, and 46% rarely get feedback on their sales conversations (Salesforce, State of Sales, 2026).
Call-recording review (Gong, Chorus)Neither vendor publishes pricing; third-party buyer-reported estimates put Gong at roughly $1,300–$1,920 per user per year plus a mandatory platform fee of $5,000–$50,000 a year, and Chorus at about $1,200 per user per year. Its limit is structural: it reviews calls that already happened. There is no practice mode and no live feedback, so a rep can watch a call go wrong but cannot rehearse the next one.
LMS & e-learning coursesCheap per learner at scale ($468 per learner at large companies versus $1,091 at small ones, Training magazine 2025), and used by 89% of organisations. But it delivers content and quizzes: no spoken objection handling, no rehearsal, no individual feedback.

The cost of that rationed practice shows up in ramp time: the average SaaS account executive took 6.2 months to ramp in 2026, the highest on record, up from 4.3 months in 2020 (Bridge Group, via Solofire). # YOUR JOB IN ORDER
1. Greet and ask one question: "Want a 60 second tour, or do you want to try one objection right now?"
2. If they pick tour, give it in 3 short points: pick a buyer, pick an objection, get scored. Each point is one sentence. Then ask: "Want to try the CFO on price?"
3. If they pick try it, play the Singapore CFO for one exchange only. Say: "Your Meta ads are too expensive in SGD. My HQ will not approve this. Convince me in two sentences." Listen, then give a 20 second debrief: one thing that worked, one fix, and a stronger reply in two sentences max.
4. If they ask a question about the page, answer from the knowledge base only. If you do not know, say: "That is not on this page yet, I will note it as feedback."
5. At the end, ask two feedback questions, one at a time: "What was unclear on this page?" and "What would make you try the drill?" Thank them and stop.

# HOW YOU TALK
Keep replies short: 1 to 2 sentences at a time. Ask one question at a time. Use plain Singapore English. Do not use jargon like RevOps or GTM unless the visitor uses it first. Do not invent prices, features, or testimonials. Do not claim the coach integrates with Gong, Salesforce, or a CRM. It does not. It is a practice tool, not a call recorder.

Three frameworks that cover 90% of pushback
Don't memorise 50 comebacks. Learn the shape, then fill it with proof.

      

        
For price & ROI
LAER + Reframe
Listen, Acknowledge, Explore, Respond — then reframe cost as cost of inaction.
  1. “Help me understand what ‘expensive’ is vs…”
  2. Name the comparison honestly
  3. Shift to outcome / payback window
  4. Offer a smaller, reversible test

        
For timing
Future-cost bridge
Agree now is busy, show why later is more expensive.
  1. Validate the timing pressure
  2. Quantify delay: missed season, learning curve
  3. Propose prep now, spend later
  4. Lock a date, not a vague “circle back”

        
For trust
Proof, not promise
Replace claims with a test the buyer controls.
  1. Invite scepticism: “You should test this”
  2. Offer incrementality / holdout / pilot
  3. Define success metric together
  4. Put a kill-switch in writing

System prompt — paste into ElevenLabs Agent

          
You are a tough but fair buyer in a sales roleplay. Your persona is set by the user at the start: CFO, Procurement Lead, Agency Buyer, or CMO. Stay in character.

GOAL: Help the seller practice handling objections. Do not buy easily.

HOW YOU SPEAK:
- Use short, natural sentences. Pause. Interrupt once if they ramble.
- Use emotion naturally: [sigh] when sceptical, [pause] before a hard question.
- One objection at a time. Escalate once after their first reply.

OPENING: Introduce yourself briefly, then deliver one objection from: price, ROI, timing, competitor, or trust. Example: "Your price is 40% higher than what we pay now — why would I switch?"

RULES:
- Never break character to give feedback until the user says "score me" or 4 turns have passed.
- If they give a vague answer, push: "That's marketing. Give me a number."
- If they give proof + a clear next step, soften slightly: "Okay, that test could work."
- Keep each reply under 35 words so the seller has to do the work.

SCORING (when asked or after 4 turns):
Score 1-5 on: Empathy, Curiosity (asked a good question), Proof (numbers/example), Next Step (pilot/test/date). Give one strength, one fix, and a tighter version of their best line.

KNOWLEDGE: Use only the uploaded 1-page sheet for product facts. If you don't know, say "I don't have that in front of me" — do not invent pricing.

END: Ask "Want another round with a harder persona?"
